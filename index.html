Crossing the Blue Mountains: a story

A short telling of the 1813 crossing, drawn from the journals of the crossing party and from what happened after. It is not a record, and it is not told as the voice of Darug, Gundungurra or Wiradjuri people.

Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander readers are advised this page names a person who has died.

Leaving South Creek, 11 May 1813

On Tuesday 11 May 1813, Gregory Blaxland, William Lawson and William Charles Wentworth left Blaxland’s farm at South Creek, near present-day St Marys. Four servants went with them, four horses laden with provisions, and five dogs.

That afternoon they crossed the Nepean River at the ford by Emu Island, and camped at the foot of the first ridge. In the days that followed they kept to the ridges, instead of trying to force a way up the valleys as earlier parties had. As they went they cut marks into the trees, so they could find the same path back.

Looking for grass

They were looking for grass. Drought in 1812 and 1813, worn pasture around Sydney, and the limits of the coastal grants had left the colony short of grass for its stock. New grazing land — not gold, and not a river to the sea — was the prize they had come for.

Traces on the western side

After they came down the mountain later called Mount York, the journals record signs that people had been on the western side of the mountains the day before: fires left behind, flowers of the honeysuckle tree scattered on the ground, and stones used to sharpen spears. The journals do not record a meeting, or an attack, or what those people decided to do.

The view west, 31 May

On 31 May they climbed a sugar-loaf hill later named Mount Blaxland. From the top they looked out on forest and grass land which, in their judgement, was enough “to support the stock of the colony for the next thirty years.”

William Cox built a road along the same ridge line between July 1814 and January 1815. On 7 May 1815 Governor Macquarie founded Bathurst — the first European settlement west of the Blue Mountains. In the years that followed, settlers took up sheep country on Wiradjuri land. In August 1824, during the Bathurst War, Governor Brisbane declared martial law west of the mountains. Wiradjuri resistance in that war included leaders such as Windradyne.

The party had found a way across. What “opening the interior” opened up is the question the case takes up.

What is true, and what is imagined

A short telling has to choose what to put in a scene. Here is where the line sits, so the two can be held apart.

The record

  • Blaxland, Lawson and Wentworth left Blaxland’s farm at South Creek on 11 May 1813, crossed the Nepean at the ford by Emu Island that afternoon, and on 31 May climbed the sugar-loaf hill later called Mount Blaxland. Four servants, four horses and five dogs went with them.
  • They were looking for new grazing land, after drought in 1812–1813, worn pasture around Sydney, and the limits of the coastal grants.
  • The judgement that the grass would “support the stock of the colony for the next thirty years” is in Blaxland’s journal.
  • The journals record fires left the day before, honeysuckle flowers, and stones used to sharpen spears, on the western side of the mountains. They do not record a meeting or an attack.
  • William Cox built a road along the same ridge between July 1814 and January 1815. Governor Macquarie founded Bathurst on 7 May 1815 — the first European settlement west of the Blue Mountains. In August 1824, during the Bathurst War, martial law was declared west of the mountains.

The telling

  • The shape of these four scenes, and any colour not found in the journals, is a writer’s arrangement — not evidence.
  • The paintings are an artist’s impression of the ridge, the traces and the plains. They are not historical photographs or records.
  • This story does not invent the thoughts, choices or words of Darug, Gundungurra or Wiradjuri people. The history of this Country belongs to them.

Darug people lived east of the mountains, Gundungurra people through the mountains, and Wiradjuri people west of them. Those are classroom outlines of Country, not hard border lines.
